Subject: [isabelle] Experiences with Isabelle primer workshops for newcomers?

Hello everybody,

My chair has convinced me (as someone with a little bit of experience with Isabelle) to hold a primer workshop for my colleagues (most of whom have no prior experience with Isabelle at all). Our research area is IT security.

My current plan is to do a quick primer with simple examples (ideally with a few hands-on examples), then work toward a bit more complex examples (particularly regarding security, e.g. how to formulate security properties in a formal language). After a break, I would like to showcase basic program verification using standard Hoare logic, as well as the Needham-Schroeder protocol using Paulson's inductive analysis. I think for both showcases the first-fail-then-fix-and-prove approach would be nice.

Has anyone run similar workshops for complete newcomers and can share what worked and what didn't? Is my plan realistic for a 1-3h workshop? Also: how much time to plan for installation problems and the hands-on parts? Are apply-style proofs or Isar proofs generally better received, and what are common stumbling blocks?

Also, could anyone recommend some state-of-the-art security verifications done in Isabelle that are suitable to showcase?

I would appreciate any advice or pointers :)

Best,
Tassilo

P.S. I am (still) a student myself, so I don't anticipate major scientific expectations from the visitors, but I want the workshop to be solid nonetheless.

Dear Tassilo,

my experience is more about teachin Isabelle/HOL to master students
ranging from apps developpers, cybersecurity and cryptographers.

However, I may suggest some subjects from easy to more engaged and
related to security:

1) Programming an equivalence check on security policies of Firewalls.
Security policies are ordered rules. Their application follows this
order and deciding the equivalence of 2 policies is important (e.g.
after a modification/reordering of rules) and is not trivial:

(https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289619483_Mignis_A_Semantic_Based_Tool_for_Firewall_
Configuration)

Depending on the security rules you consider you can tune the difficulty
of the problem.

2) Proving safety of a static analyser on a basic programming
language/bytecode virtual machine. Again depending on the language you
consider and static analysis you perform, difficulty is tunable.

3) Programming a security policy over PKCS#11 API, i.e., programming the
application that accepts or reject the wrap/unwrap/encrypt/decrypt commands.

https://people.irisa.fr/Stephanie.Delaune/PUBLICATIONS/DKS-jcs09.pdf

If you need I have material on 1), 2), not yet on 3), I am still trying
to find the right way to make the student formalize the problem in a
limited amount of time :-)

Best,

Thomas

In August I did a 3h tutorial (2 times 90min). I asked people to have
Isabelle already installed (as usual some, but not all followed these
instructions).
 I deliberately designed it as "hands-on" with a lot of exercises. I
did follow the appraoch of developing a deep embedding for a simple
while-language up to "sketching" the development of a VCG including
demonstrating Eisbach, developing a new top-level command (essentially a
specific setup for values, alloing to run and debug while programs). The
last 15min or so I used for demonstrating Isabelle projects that we are
locally developing.

My goal was to show Isabelle and its features to people and give them
enough to start working with it (and allow them to find documentation
and help for what they want to do).

Still, it was quite dense and it only "worked" because I worked a lot
with templates provided to people so that they only had to fill out the
gaps.  For a proper tutorial, I would clearly aim for either less
content or more time.

Has anyone run similar workshops for complete newcomers and can share
what worked and what didn't? Is my plan realistic for a 1-3h workshop?
Also: how much time to plan for installation problems and the hands-on
parts? Are apply-style proofs or Isar proofs generally better
received, and what are common stumbling blocks?

Also, could anyone recommend some state-of-the-art security
verifications done in Isabelle that are suitable to showcase?

I demonstrated our security protocol verification tool, called PSPSP
(https://isa-afp.org/entries/Automated_Stateful_Protocol_Verification.html),
but as co-author I am heavily biased ;-).

From: Lawrence Paulson <lp15@cam.ac.uk>

Dear Tassilo,

Although I fondly remember that old work on protocol verification, it is quite outdated. It leads to messy proofs. I hope someone can suggest a more modern verification example.

What goes into a tutorial changes drastically with technology. In the 1990s, I spent quite a lot of time going over basic libraries (integers!) and the facts in them because users had to construct their proofs painstakingly, mentioning these facts by name. But then Isar and Sledgehammer came along: suddenly you could prove many difficult theorems simply by writing out a proof skeleton in Isar, using sledgehammer to fill the gaps, refining the skeleton if the gaps were too big. Then your main task was to teach Isar itself, including inductive proofs and other techniques beyond the scope of Sledgehammer.

Now, love it or hate it, we are in the AI age. Students will still need to understand the Isabelle/HOL formalism: sorts, types and type classes, terms, formulas, locales and how to read proofs. They will probably be intending much more ambitious projects than in the past. So an overview of some of the key components of the AFP also becomes necessary.

Larry Paulson
